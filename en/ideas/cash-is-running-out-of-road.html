Cash is running out of road in Latin America

Digitalization has already changed how we pay tolls, gasoline and parking. This is what has already won, what is still stuck and the challenges that remain.

Ten years ago, everything on a highway in Latin America was cash. You stopped at the toll booth, dug for bills, paid for gasoline in cash and paid for parking the same way. Nobody asked whether there was another way, because there wasn’t.

Today, in some countries, cash no longer exists on the highway. In others, it is still the rule. And in between sits an entire region in transition, with three settings: the toll booth, the gas station and the parking lot.

In this article, with data, I walk through that transition: where it has already beaten cash, where it is winning, where it is still stuck, and what challenges remain.

Where digitalization has already won

The first thing that struck me traveling through Chile and Uruguay was what didn’t happen. On the road between Santiago de Chile and Viña del Mar and Valparaíso, and between Montevideo and Punta del Este, you don’t have to stop at a toll booth. The car goes through, the tag charges, and the trip continues. Anyone who has to pay in cash has to detour, take more time and pay more. The tag is the rule; cash is the exception.

Uruguay. It is the complete case.

  • On May 1, 2024, cash was eliminated at the last two tolls that still accepted it. Today 100% of collection is electronic: TAG or license-plate reading (SUCIVE). On the Interbalnearia, the Pando and Solís tolls are tag-only.[1]
  • There is a direct incentive: the toll with TAG costs $142.98 Uruguayan pesos versus $185 via SUCIVE, 23% less.[1]
  • In May 2016 it banned cash at gas stations in Montevideo during nighttime hours, and the measure was extended until cash was eliminated all day.
  • A study published in the Journal of Law and Economics (Gandelman, Munyo and Schertz, 2023) measured the effect with georeferenced crime data: robberies in the treated areas fell 25% or more, without robberies shifting to other hours or places.[8]
  • It is the region’s most solid evidence: taking cash out is not a technological whim; it is a security measure that can be measured.

Chile. It won in the city.

  • Santiago’s urban highways have run on TAG since 2004, among the first urban free-flow systems in the world.[2]
  • On Route 68, barrier-free electronic collection began in December 2018.[3]
  • Drivers without a TAG are not locked out: they go through and pay later online, but with a surcharge - 1.25 times the tariff on Route 68 and double on Santiago’s urban highways.[3]
  • The message is clear: you can go without a tag, but it costs you more and takes more time.

Buenos Aires.

  • The city’s highways already bill around 97% through TelePASE.[6]
  • The last cash booths were removed between May and June 2026, and from November 2026 everything is free-flow.[6]
  • In six years, cash disappeared from Buenos Aires highways.

Where it is winning, but has not won

In the rest of the region, digitalization is halfway there. The infrastructure exists. What is missing is for people to use it.

Mexico. It is the perfect example of the gap.

  • 58.2% of toll revenue is collected by electronic tolling, but only around 34% of users have a TAG, even though more than 90% of lanes already have the infrastructure.[4]
  • CAPUFE sums it up in seconds: crossing with a TAG takes around 1 second; paying in cash, up to 30.[4]
  • The infrastructure is there. The habit is not.
  • And watch a figure circulating in the press: it is not true that the TAG will be mandatory by year-end. The guidelines in force since July 2026 say it is not mandatory and that multimodal booths keep cash.[4]

Colombia. It has the supply without the demand.

  • 91% of tolls offer electronic payment, but only 30% of vehicles use it.[5]
  • In Antioquia it reaches 52%, and at peak hours in Las Palmas and Copacabana, 70%.[5]
  • The system’s fragility was exposed in August 2026, when electronic payment was suspended in Copacabana and Las Palmas because of a concession change: overnight, cash only.[5]

Brazil. It is advancing from another angle: free-flow.

  • 65% of traffic on concessioned highways already uses a TAG, there are some 15 million vehicles with a tag, and gantry collection operates on 13 concessions.[7]
  • In the BR-101 pilot, 67.6% of payments were by tag and 28.5% by Pix.[7]
  • The tag is winning, but Pix - instant payments - is winning faster.

Where it is still stuck: gasoline and parking

If toll booths are the front where digitalization is winning, there are two fronts where it is still stuck.

Gasoline. Cash is supposed to be accepted because it is cheaper than other payment methods. At gas stations, that account is incomplete. I set out to understand how gasoline moves in the region, and what I found is that cash also has a cost: safes, cash-in-transit companies, counting, shrinkage and theft. Global studies put the total cost of handling cash between 1.3% and 9% of cash revenue, versus a lower cost for cards. Nobody publishes[15] what a cash-in-transit company charges in Latin America, so that part of the account remains a mystery.

And even so, Mexico is the hardest case: only around 7% of gas-station transactions are paid by card, according to Banxico (2025). Card fees[9] run from 1.9% to 3.5%, and up to 5% at the smallest stations. Mexico has some 14,000[9] gas stations and cash accounts for about a third of the value sold, some $28,600 million pesos a month. Chile and Argentina[9] are heading down the same path as Uruguay - a bill in Chile to ban cash at night, regulated self-service in Argentina - but in Mexico, cash at the pump is still the rule.[10]

Parking, the last holdout. When I think about which use cases still force us to keep cash, parking always comes to mind. There are already companies automating it, but it does not work everywhere.

Even Estapar, the most digital operator in the region, still generates around 75% of its revenue through non-digital channels. Parking is where[12] cash resists the most, and it is the best thermometer of what is still missing.

The challenges that remain

If digitalization has already won in some places and is winning in others, why does it get stuck in the rest? There are five challenges that repeat across the region.

1. Inclusion: half the population has no account. Only 53% of adults in Mexico have a bank account, and only 14.2% have a credit card. In Colombia, 57.1% have an account. In Brazil, 86.4%. A 100% digital system[12] excludes almost half the population in Mexico and Colombia. Digitalization without inclusion is not modernization: it is a steeper toll for the same people as always.

2. Delinquency: when the barrier disappears, non-payment appears. In Chile, uncollectible TAG accounts rose from 3.34% in 2016 to around 9%. In Brazil, in the BR-101[16] free-flow pilot, about 7% of revenue went unpaid. Cash toll booths[7], for all their slowness, collected at the moment of passage. Electronic collection charges later, and sometimes does not collect at all.

3. Fragility: the digital system also goes down. In August 2026, electronic payment was suspended at two Medellín tolls because of a concession change. When the app fails[5] or the code does not read, cash is the plan B. And a system without a plan B is a fragile system.

4. Costs: cash is expensive, but so is the card. Card fees at Mexican gas stations reach 5% at small stations, on already thin margins. The government[9], banks and card issuers agreed to take interchange to zero for gas stations between May and October 2026, and the benefit expired on October 31 with no confirmed renewal. Digitizing[9] without resolving who pays the fee is shifting the cost, not eliminating it.

5. Crime does not disappear; it moves. Brazil is the warning: Operation Hidden Carbon (August 2025) found that more than 1,000 gas stations in 10 states moved R$52,000 million between 2020 and 2024 laundering organized-crime money through fintechs. Digital payment without oversight[11] does not eliminate crime: it moves it from the holdup at the window to laundering in the system.

What's next: Mexico and the Digital Economy Law

And this is where the story gets interesting, because Mexico is about to force what in Chile and Uruguay happened through evolution and through law.

The Ley de Economía Digital para Pagos Digitales y Electrónicos (Digital Economy Law for Digital and Electronic Payments) was published in the Official Gazette on November 4, 2026 and entered into force on November 5. The Chamber of Deputies approved it by 330 votes in favor and 122 against, and the Senate by 73 to 34. President Claudia Sheinbaum[13] proposed it as part of the 2027 Economic Package.

What the law does - and what it does not do - is key to understanding what comes next:

  • It does not mention toll booths or gas stations by name. It does not ban cash in any sector directly.[13]
  • It gives the Finance Ministry (SHCP) the power to designate "strategic sectors" where digital payment is the only form accepted. The government has said publicly that gas stations and toll booths would be the first sectors.[13]
  • That designation has not yet been published. SHCP has 15 business days from entry into force, with an estimated deadline between November 26 and 29, 2026. Until it is published, nothing changes at the booth or at the pump.[13]
  • There is a safeguard: if there is a contingency - a power failure, a banking-system outage - cash can be used. But the contingency cannot be invoked permanently.[13]
  • The official reason for gas stations is not only modernization: it is traceability. Eliminating cash in gasoline, together with traceability of Mexico's special excise tax on fuel (IEPS), aims to end fuel theft and illegal imports. The president put it this way: cash will be eliminated in gasoline payments because that helps end huachicol, Mexico's illegal fuel trade.[13]

The stakes are high, and so are the risks. 85.2% of people use cash for purchases of $500 pesos or less. Only 63% of adults have a debit card and 29% a credit card. Some 200 gas stations[14] are outside guaranteed mobile coverage. And transport companies warn of a cash-flow mismatch: they would have to pay for fuel and tolls digitally while they keep collecting fares in cash. Amparos (constitutional injunctions) before the Supreme Court are already anticipated.

But the direction is clear. Mexico already has the infrastructure - electronic tolling on more than 90% of lanes, CoDi, SPEI, the 2018 Fintech Law - and now it has the law. What is missing is the decision[4], sector by sector. And that decision, if it comes, will change the highway faster than any public works project: nothing new has to be built, you just have to stop stopping.

My recommendations

  1. Make the tag the rule, not the exception. Incentives work better than mandates: let paying in cash cost more and take more time, as it already does in Santiago and Montevideo - Uruguay charges 23% less with TAG[1] and Chile announced discounts of 10% to 25% on Autopista Central.
  2. Let cash pay its real cost. Every cash stop costs time for everyone behind. The surcharge for late or manual payment is not a punishment: it is the price of the friction it creates.
  3. At gas stations, safety before habit. The Uruguay case is measured: 25% fewer robberies. The debate[8] is not whether cash is cheaper, but for whom and at what cost. And the cost is paid by workers on the night shift.
  4. Parking: one way to pay, or none. As long as every city and every operator has its own app, cash will remain the plan B. Interoperability - the same tag working for the toll and the parking lot - is what turned the tag into the rule in Chile. That is the model.
